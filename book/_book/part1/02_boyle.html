3  Compressible Gases

From Pressure to Volume

4 Engineering Question

NoteEngineering Question

Why can a diver become increasingly buoyant during an ascent without adding any air?

At first sight, the answer appears obvious.

The diver is surrounded by water, and water is essentially incompressible. If the amount of water does not change, why should buoyancy change?

The answer lies elsewhere.

The most important component of the underwater environment is not the water itself, but the gas trapped inside the diver’s equipment and lungs.

Unlike water, gases are highly compressible.

As pressure changes with depth, gas volume changes accordingly.

Those changes in volume alter buoyancy, and buoyancy alters motion.

This chapter explores one of the most elegant relationships in classical physics:

Pressure does not directly change buoyancy.

Pressure changes volume, and volume changes buoyancy.

In this chapter we focus only on the first half of that chain:

Pressure → Volume

The second half,

Volume → Buoyant Force,

will be developed in the following chapter through Archimedes’ Principle.

5 Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain why gases behave differently from liquids;
  • describe Robert Boyle’s contribution to gas physics;
  • state Boyle’s Law;
  • explain why absolute pressure must be used in gas calculations;
  • predict how gas volume changes during descent and ascent;
  • understand why pressure produces nonlinear changes in volume;
  • prepare for the introduction of Archimedes’ Principle in the next chapter.

6 A Simple Experiment

Imagine holding an empty plastic bottle sealed with a small amount of air.

At the surface its shape appears perfectly normal.

Now imagine taking that bottle to a depth of 10 metres.

Nothing has happened to the plastic itself.

Nothing has happened to the surrounding water.

Yet the bottle begins to deform because the trapped air occupies a smaller volume.

When you return to the surface, the bottle expands again.

The bottle has become a simple pressure sensor.

ImportantDiveLab Insight

Pressure acts on gases by changing their volume.

Understanding this relationship is the first step toward understanding buoyancy.

7 Why Gases Behave Differently

Water is only weakly compressible under recreational diving conditions.

Air is highly compressible.

That single difference explains why lung volume, bubbles and buoyancy compensators continuously change during a dive.

8 Robert Boyle and the Birth of Gas Physics

Robert Boyle (1627–1691) showed that, for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related.

This relationship, known as Boyle’s Law, underpins virtually every aspect of scuba diving involving trapped gas.

Chapter 1 introduced:

\[ \text{Depth} \rightarrow \text{Pressure}. \]

This chapter extends the systems chain:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Depth} \rightarrow \text{Pressure} \rightarrow \text{Volume} } \]

The next chapter will show how volume determines buoyant force.

11 Boyle’s Law

11.1 A Fundamental Experimental Observation

Imagine a transparent syringe whose nozzle has been sealed.

Inside the syringe there is only air.

Initially, the piston can move freely.

As we push the piston inward, something interesting happens.

The force required to continue compressing the air becomes progressively larger.

The air resists compression.

If we release the piston, the air expands again, returning almost to its original volume.

Nothing has been added to the syringe.

Nothing has been removed.

Only two quantities have changed:

  • the pressure inside the syringe;
  • the volume occupied by the air.

This simple experiment reveals one of the most important properties of gases:

Increasing pressure decreases volume.

The relationship is not arbitrary.

It follows a remarkably simple mathematical law discovered by Robert Boyle.

11.2 Boyle’s Law

For a fixed amount of gas maintained at constant temperature,

the product of pressure and volume remains constant.

Mathematically,

\[ PV = \text{constant}. \]

Equivalently, comparing two different states of the same gas,

\[ P_1V_1=P_2V_2. \]

This equation is known as Boyle’s Law.

Although deceptively simple, it explains nearly every phenomenon involving trapped gas during a recreational dive.

ImportantDiveLab Insight

Boyle’s Law does not describe the behaviour of water.

It describes the behaviour of gases.

Whenever a diver breathes, inflates a buoyancy compensator, observes bubbles, or carries an air space underwater, Boyle’s Law is at work.

11.3 Where Does Boyle’s Law Come From?

Boyle originally discovered the relationship experimentally.

Today we can derive it from the Ideal Gas Law,

\[ PV=nRT, \]

where

  • (P) is the absolute pressure;
  • (V) is the gas volume;
  • (n) is the number of moles of gas;
  • (R) is the universal gas constant;
  • (T) is the absolute temperature.

During a typical diving manoeuvre, we assume that

  • the amount of gas remains constant;
  • the temperature changes only slightly.

Under these assumptions,

(n), (R), and (T) may all be considered constant.

Therefore,

\[ PV=\text{constant}, \]

which is precisely Boyle’s Law.

This derivation also reveals the assumptions behind the model.

Boyle’s Law is not a universal law valid under every circumstance.

It is an excellent approximation for many recreational diving situations.

11.4 An Inverse Relationship

Boyle’s Law tells us that pressure and volume vary in opposite directions.

If pressure doubles,

volume becomes half as large.

If pressure triples,

volume becomes one third of its original value.

More generally,

\[ V=\frac{K}{P}, \]

where (K) is a constant.

The relationship is therefore inverse, not linear.

This distinction is extremely important.

Pressure increases almost linearly with depth.

Volume does not.

Consequently, equal changes in depth do not produce equal changes in volume.

That observation will become one of the key ideas of this chapter.

11.5 Engineering Perspective

Control engineers often ask two questions.

  1. What is the input?

In this chapter,

the input is pressure.

  1. What is the output?

The output is gas volume.

The system therefore transforms

\[ \text{Pressure} \longrightarrow \text{Volume}. \]

In later chapters,

this volume will generate buoyancy,

and buoyancy will generate motion.

Each chapter adds one new link to the chain.

The complete diver will eventually emerge as an interconnected dynamical system.

11.6 Looking Ahead

Boyle’s Law tells us how pressure changes volume.

The next question is equally important.

How much does pressure actually increase underwater?

To answer that question we must distinguish between

  • atmospheric pressure,
  • hydrostatic pressure,
  • and absolute pressure.

Only absolute pressure can be used in Boyle’s Law.

The next section explains why.
