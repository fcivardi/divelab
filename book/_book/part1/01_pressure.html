2  Pressure and Depth

The Surface Paradox

3 Engineering Question

NoteEngineering Question

Where does pressure change most dramatically during a dive?

The intuitive answer is often: at great depth.

That answer is only partly correct.

Absolute pressure continues to increase as a diver descends, but the largest relative change occurs immediately below the surface. This distinction between absolute change and relative change is the key to understanding why the first metres of a dive are so important.

This chapter develops that idea carefully. We begin with physical intuition, derive the hydrostatic-pressure equation, examine its assumptions, and connect it to the broader systems story of DiveLab.

4 Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain why pressure increases with depth;
  • derive the hydrostatic-pressure equation;
  • distinguish gauge pressure from absolute pressure;
  • interpret the pressure gradient physically;
  • explain why pressure is linear with depth;
  • calculate absolute pressure at common diving depths;
  • distinguish absolute pressure change from fractional pressure change;
  • explain why the largest relative pressure change occurs near the surface;
  • connect pressure changes to compressible gas volumes and buoyancy;
  • use the companion notebook to reproduce and extend the chapter results.

5 A Simple Observation

Imagine descending from the surface to a depth of 10 metres in seawater.

At the surface, the diver is already exposed to atmospheric pressure. At approximately 10 metres, the surrounding absolute pressure is about twice the surface value.

The next 10 metres, from 10 to 20 metres, add roughly the same absolute pressure increment. However, the pressure does not double again. It rises from approximately 2 bar absolute to approximately 3 bar absolute.

The same absolute increment therefore produces a smaller relative change.

This is the surface paradox:

ImportantDiveLab Insight

Hydrostatic pressure increases linearly with depth, but its fractional increase is greatest near the surface.

6 Pressure as Force per Unit Area

Pressure measures how a force is distributed over an area:

\[ P = \frac{F}{A}. \tag{6.1}\]

where:

  • \(P\) is pressure;
  • \(F\) is the force normal to the surface;
  • \(A\) is the area over which the force acts.

The SI unit of pressure is the pascal:

\[ 1\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}. \]

In diving practice, pressure is also commonly expressed in bar:

\[ 1\ \mathrm{bar} = 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}. \]

The atmosphere at sea level is approximately:

\[ P_0 \approx 1.01325 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 1.01325\ \mathrm{bar}. \]

For many introductory calculations, this is rounded to:

\[ P_0 \approx 1\ \mathrm{bar}. \]

7 Why Pressure Increases with Depth

Consider a vertical column of water with:

  • cross-sectional area \(A\);
  • height \(h\);
  • density \(\rho\);
  • gravitational acceleration \(g\).

The water column has volume:

\[ V = Ah. \]

Its mass is:

\[ m = \rho V = \rho Ah. \]

Its weight is:

\[ W = mg = \rho Ahg. \]

That weight acts over the area \(A\), so the additional pressure caused by the water column is:

\[ \Delta P = \frac{W}{A} = \frac{\rho Ahg}{A}. \]

The area cancels:

\[ \boxed{\Delta P = \rho gh} \tag{7.1}\]

If the surface pressure is \(P_0\), then the absolute pressure at depth \(h\) is:

\[ \boxed{P(h) = P_0 + \rho gh} \tag{7.2}\]

This is the hydrostatic-pressure equation.

TipPhysical Interpretation

The increase in pressure is caused by the weight of the water above the diver. The deeper the diver, the taller the water column and the greater the pressure.

8 Differential Form

The same result can be written locally.

For a fluid at rest, vertical force balance gives:

\[ \frac{dP}{dh} = \rho g. \tag{8.1}\]

If \(\rho\) and \(g\) are constant, integration gives:

\[ P(h) - P_0 = \int_0^h \rho g\,d\eta = \rho gh. \]

Therefore:

\[ P(h) = P_0 + \rho gh. \]

The derivative \(dP/dh\) is the pressure gradient.

For seawater with \(\rho \approx 1025\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\):

\[ \frac{dP}{dh} \approx 1025 \times 9.81 \approx 1.01 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}}. \]

So each metre of seawater adds roughly:

\[ 0.10\ \mathrm{bar}. \]

9 Gauge Pressure and Absolute Pressure

Two pressure conventions are common.

9.1 Gauge Pressure

Gauge pressure measures pressure relative to the atmosphere:

\[ P_{\mathrm{gauge}} = P - P_0. \]

Using Equation 7.2:

\[ P_{\mathrm{gauge}} = \rho gh. \]

At the surface, \(P_{\mathrm{gauge}} = 0\).

9.2 Absolute Pressure

Absolute pressure is measured relative to vacuum:

\[ P_{\mathrm{abs}} = P_0 + \rho gh. \]

At the surface, \(P_{\mathrm{abs}} = P_0\).

For gas-volume calculations, absolute pressure must be used.

WarningCommon Misconception

Boyle’s law cannot be applied using gauge pressure. A gauge pressure of zero at the surface does not mean that the gas is under zero pressure.

10 A Practical Diving Approximation

In seawater, pressure increases by about 1 bar every 10 metres.

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{P_{\mathrm{abs}}(h) \approx 1 + \frac{h}{10}} \tag{10.1}\]

with \(P_{\mathrm{abs}}\) in bar and \(h\) in metres of seawater.

Depth Approximate absolute pressure
0 m 1 bar
5 m 1.5 bar
10 m 2 bar
20 m 3 bar
30 m 4 bar
40 m 5 bar

11 Why Pressure Is Linear with Depth

From Equation 7.2:

\[ P(h) = P_0 + \rho gh. \]

If \(P_0\), \(\rho\), and \(g\) are constant, this has the linear form:

\[ P(h) = b + mh, \]

with intercept \(b=P_0\) and slope \(m=\rho g\).

Therefore:

\[ \frac{dP}{dh} = \rho g \]

is constant.

Every additional metre adds approximately the same pressure increment.

NoteDiveLab Insight

The pressure gradient is constant in the simple hydrostatic model. The special importance of the surface does not come from a larger absolute pressure increment per metre.

The special importance of the surface appears when we examine relative change.

12 The Surface Paradox

Suppose the diver descends through a small increment \(\Delta h\).

The corresponding pressure increment is:

\[ \Delta P = \rho g\,\Delta h. \]

Under the constant-density model, this absolute increment is the same at every depth.

But the fractional increase is:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P(h)} = \frac{\rho g\,\Delta h}{P_0+\rho gh}. \tag{12.1}\]

As depth increases, the denominator becomes larger.

Therefore \(\Delta P/P(h)\) decreases with depth.

For a continuous measure, consider the relative pressure gradient:

\[ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dh}. \]

Using Equation 8.1:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dh} = \frac{\rho g}{P_0+\rho gh} } \tag{12.2}\]

This quantity is maximum at \(h=0\) and decreases monotonically as depth increases.

12.1 Numerical Example

Using the diving approximation:

  • from 0 m to 10 m, pressure rises from 1 bar to 2 bar;
  • from 10 m to 20 m, pressure rises from 2 bar to 3 bar;
  • from 20 m to 30 m, pressure rises from 3 bar to 4 bar.

The absolute increase is 1 bar each time.

The relative increases are:

\[ \frac{2-1}{1} = 100\%, \]

\[ \frac{3-2}{2} = 50\%, \]

\[ \frac{4-3}{3} \approx 33.3\%. \]

The first 10 metres therefore produce the largest proportional increase.

ImportantEngineering Answer

The first metres of descent are not special because pressure rises faster in an absolute sense. They are special because the same pressure increment represents the largest fraction of the current pressure.

13 Why Relative Change Matters

Many physical systems respond to ratios rather than differences.

For an ideal gas at constant temperature:

\[ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2. \]

Therefore:

\[ \frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{P_1}{P_2}. \]

A change from 1 bar to 2 bar halves the volume.

A change from 2 bar to 3 bar reduces the volume to \(2/3\) of its previous value.

The first pressure interval therefore causes the larger relative volume change.

This is why the surface region becomes critical for lungs, buoyancy compensators, dry suits, gas bubbles, and buoyant force generated by compressible volumes.

The pressure law itself is linear.

The consequences are often nonlinear.

14 Assumptions of the Hydrostatic Model

The equation \(P(h)=P_0+\rho gh\) depends on several assumptions.

14.1 The Fluid Is at Rest

Hydrostatics neglects acceleration and flow effects.

14.2 Density Is Constant

Water is only weakly compressible over ordinary recreational-diving depths, so constant density is an excellent approximation.

14.3 Gravity Is Constant

Over diving depths, variation in \(g\) is negligible.

14.4 The Free-Surface Pressure Is Known

At sea level this is approximately atmospheric pressure. At altitude, \(P_0\) is lower.

14.5 Depth Is Measured Vertically

Hydrostatic pressure depends on vertical depth below the free surface, not on path length travelled.

TipModelling Principle

A model is useful when its assumptions match the question being asked. The simple hydrostatic model is extremely accurate for introductory diving calculations.

15 Sea Water and Fresh Water

Typical densities are:

\[ \rho_{\mathrm{fresh}} \approx 1000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}, \]

\[ \rho_{\mathrm{sea}} \approx 1025\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}. \]

Therefore, pressure rises slightly faster with depth in seawater.

16 Pressure as an Input to the Rest of DiveLab

Pressure is the first external variable in the DiveLab system.

It drives several later models:

  • compressible volume;
  • buoyancy;
  • gas consumption;
  • dive-computer depth estimation;
  • decompression compartments.

This gives the first systems chain in the course:

\[ \boxed{ \text{depth} \rightarrow \text{pressure} \rightarrow \text{gas volume} \rightarrow \text{buoyancy} \rightarrow \text{motion} } \]

18 Worked Examples

18.1 Absolute Pressure at 18 m

Using Equation 10.1:

\[ P_{\mathrm{abs}} \approx 1+\frac{18}{10} = 2.8\ \mathrm{bar}. \]

18.2 Gauge Pressure at 18 m

\[ P_{\mathrm{gauge}} \approx 2.8-1 = 1.8\ \mathrm{bar}. \]

18.3 Relative Pressure Change from 5 m to 10 m

At 5 m, \(P_1\approx1.5\ \mathrm{bar}\).

At 10 m, \(P_2\approx2.0\ \mathrm{bar}\).

Therefore:

\[ \frac{P_2-P_1}{P_1} = \frac{0.5}{1.5} \approx 33.3\%. \]

18.4 Relative Pressure Change from 25 m to 30 m

At 25 m, \(P_1\approx3.5\ \mathrm{bar}\).

At 30 m, \(P_2\approx4.0\ \mathrm{bar}\).

Therefore:

\[ \frac{0.5}{3.5} \approx 14.3\%. \]

19 Common Misconceptions

19.1 “Pressure increases faster near the surface”

Not in the constant-density hydrostatic model. The absolute gradient \(dP/dh=\rho g\) is constant. What decreases with depth is the fractional gradient.

19.2 “Pressure is zero at the surface”

Gauge pressure is zero at the surface. Absolute pressure is approximately 1 bar.

19.3 “Depth travelled determines pressure”

Only vertical depth below the free surface matters.

19.4 “A 1 bar increase always has the same effect”

It has the same absolute magnitude, but not the same relative magnitude.

21 Key Equations

\[ \Delta P=\rho gh \]

\[ \frac{dP}{dh}=\rho g \]

\[ P_{\mathrm{abs}}(h)\approx1+\frac{h}{10} \]

22 Key Ideas

  • Pressure increases because of the weight of the water above the diver.
  • Hydrostatic pressure is linear with depth under constant-density assumptions.
  • Gauge pressure and absolute pressure are different.
  • Gas laws require absolute pressure.
  • The absolute pressure increment per metre is approximately constant.
  • The fractional pressure increment is greatest near the surface.
  • Linear pressure variation can drive nonlinear physical consequences.
  • Pressure is the first input to the DiveLab systems chain.

23 Looking Ahead

Pressure by itself is linear and predictable.

But many of the volumes affected by pressure are compressible.

In the next chapter, we introduce Boyle’s law:

\[ P_1V_1=P_2V_2. \]

That relation transforms a simple linear pressure law into nonlinear changes in volume, buoyancy, and eventually vertical motion.

The next question is therefore unavoidable:

What happens to a trapped gas volume when the diver changes depth?

1.8\ \mathrm{bar}.
$$

## Relative Pressure Change from 5 m to 10 m

At 5 m, $P_1\approx1.5\ \mathrm{bar}$.

At 10 m, $P_2\approx2.0\ \mathrm{bar}$.

Therefore:

$$
\frac{P_2-P_1}{P_1}
=
\frac{0.5}{1.5}
\approx
33.3\%.
$$

## Relative Pressure Change from 25 m to 30 m

At 25 m, $P_1\approx3.5\ \mathrm{bar}$.

At 30 m, $P_2\approx4.0\ \mathrm{bar}$.

Therefore:

$$
\frac{0.5}{3.5}
\approx
14.3\%.
$$

# Common Misconceptions

## “Pressure increases faster near the surface”

Not in the constant-density hydrostatic model. The absolute gradient $dP/dh=\rho g$ is constant. What decreases with depth is the fractional gradient.

## “Pressure is zero at the surface”

Gauge pressure is zero at the surface. Absolute pressure is approximately 1 bar.

## “Depth travelled determines pressure”

Only vertical depth below the free surface matters.

## “A 1 bar increase always has the same effect”

It has the same absolute magnitude, but not the same relative magnitude.

# Key Ideas

- Pressure increases because of the weight of the water above the diver.
- Hydrostatic pressure is linear with depth under constant-density assumptions.
- Gauge pressure and absolute pressure are different.
- Gas laws require absolute pressure.
- The absolute pressure increment per metre is approximately constant.
- The fractional pressure increment is greatest near the surface.
- Linear pressure variation can drive nonlinear physical consequences.
- Pressure is the first input to the DiveLab systems chain.
